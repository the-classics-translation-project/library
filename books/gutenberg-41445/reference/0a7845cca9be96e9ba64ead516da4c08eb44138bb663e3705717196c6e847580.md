# *Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus* — Work-wide translator reference

**Basis and limits.** This guide covers **all 38 supplied source nodes, n0001–n0038**, including the closing Walton continuation and `THE END.` It is a continuity and interpretation aid for translators, **not** a substitute for the prose, a chapter summary, a term database, or an independently collated edition. Node references below identify the supplied structure, not pages. Statements about the story are distinguished where necessary from characters' beliefs, editorial interpretations, and unresolved textual problems.

## 1. Textual identity and navigation

The supplied work is *Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus*, internally divided into **three volumes**, with **four opening letters** and **23 chapters** whose numbering **restarts in each volume** (7 + 9 + 7). The narrative then returns to Walton in a separately labeled continuation. When referring to a chapter, always identify its **volume** as well as its number.

| Part of supplied text | Source nodes | What to preserve |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Root title, Volume I title matter, preface | `n0001–n0004` | The subtitle; London imprint and **1818** date; *Paradise Lost* epigraph; dedication to **William Godwin**; preface; repeated half-title. |
| Walton's opening four letters | `n0005–n0008` | Letters I–IV to Mrs. Saville; their dates, addresses, signatures; the successive dated journal entries *inside* Letter IV. |
| Volume I, Chapters I–VII | `n0009–n0016` | Repeated half-title followed by Victor's narrative; chapter headings I–VII. |
| End of Volume I | `n0017` | Explicit `END OF VOL. I.` marker. |
| Volume II, Chapters I–IX | `n0018–n0027` | Repeated volume title/imprint/epigraph; headings I–IX; creature's extended first-person story within Victor's narration. |
| End of Volume II | `n0028` | Explicit `END OF VOL. II.` marker. |

**Provenance caution.** The supplied source notes identify the Gutenberg #41445 acquisition with the **1818** three-volume text and report the provider's description of a photo-reprint. The title matter itself prints 1818 (`n0002`, `n0018`, `n0029`), but this guide has not independently verified the physical exemplar or compared other editions. The notes name Mary Wollstonecraft Shelley in provider metadata; the printed dedication ends **“BY THE AUTHOR”** rather than displaying a modern author name (`n0002`). The preface speaks in an authorial voice about the summer of **1816** near Geneva and a ghost-story exercise; that date belongs to the preface's account of composition, **not** to the fictional chronology (`n0003`). The preface explicitly resists treating characters' philosophical opinions as necessarily the author's.

## 2. Narrators, embedded documents, and who knows what

The narrative is **nested**, and an inner voice should never silently become an omniscient account:

1. **Robert Walton**, an English sea captain, writes to his married sister **Margaret Saville** in England. His letters mix practical observations, ambition, loneliness, admiration of his rescued passenger, and editorial description (`n0005–n0008`, `n0037`). His first hope that the North Pole might be warm or a realm of perpetual beauty is **his conjecture**, not a fact of this fictional world (`n0005`).
2. **Victor Frankenstein** tells Walton his retrospective history, narrated in the first person from Volume I Chapter I through Volume III Chapter VII (`n0010–n0036`). Victor is the scientist and the bearer of the surname **Frankenstein**; the being he makes is **not given a personal name**. Victor's moral reflections, claims of guilt, judgments about others, and memories of dates remain his perspective.
3. **The created being** recounts its own experiences to Victor at a mountain hut after their confrontation on the glacier. The nested account begins at **Volume II Chapter III (`n0021`)**, after Victor describes the meeting in `n0020`, and ends in **Volume II Chapter VIII (`n0026`)**. Victor resumes the primary narration in Volume II Chapter IX (`n0027`). The being remembers its own earliest sensory experiences imperfectly; it narrates its benevolent acts as well as its crimes. It is eloquent and capable of self-justification.
4. **Documents and recollections inside these accounts** include Elizabeth's letter about the household and Justine (`n0014`), Alphonse's report of William's murder (`n0015`), the creature's recollection and possession of **Safie and Felix's letters** (`n0024`), the **Victor laboratory journal** that the creature reads (`n0025`), and Elizabeth's later letter about marrying Victor (`n0034`). These voices have their own temporal viewpoints and imperfect information.
5. **Walton resumes direct narration** in `n0037`, including Victor's last speeches, the sailors' deputation, Victor's death, and an encounter with the being beside Victor's body. Walton says he made notes of Victor's story, and Victor **corrected and augmented** them, particularly the exchanges with his creature. Thus the purportedly verbatim inner dialogue has passed through at least Victor's and Walton's editorial mediation (`n0008`, `n0037`).

**Critical knowledge boundaries:**

- Victor sees the being near the site of William's death and **concludes immediately** that it must be the murderer (`n0015`); this is an inference **at that point**, later supported by the being's own confession (`n0026`). Do not back-project that confirmation into Victor's earlier degree of knowledge.
- **Elizabeth, Alphonse, Ernest, Henry**, and Justine do not share Victor's knowledge of the creation during their relevant scenes. Henry recognizes that Victor is disturbed but does **not** obtain his secret (`n0013–n0014`). Victor's family mistakenly understands his distress largely as grief or illness (`n0019`, `n0030`, `n0034`).
- Justine's **confession of guilt is false and coerced**, given under threat of religious condemnation; Elizabeth briefly believes it before Justine explains the lie (`n0016`). Treat the court's verdict and villagers' beliefs as mistaken, not narrative truth.
- The blind **De Lacey** initially responds to the creature's words without seeing its appearance; the sighted younger family returns and reacts in horror (`n0025`). The creature's earlier description of the family as its “protectors” is affectionate **self-deception**: they had not knowingly adopted or protected it (`n0023–n0025`).
- Walton hears Victor's warning not to seek the means of making life, witnesses the creature, and reports receiving supporting letters. None of this makes Walton privy to a usable scientific procedure: Victor **refuses to divulge it** (`n0012`, `n0037`). Walton is sympathetic to Victor but repeatedly judges events through his desire for a friend and a glorious expedition.

## 3. People, kinship, and relationship changes

### The Frankenstein household and close friends

- **Victor Frankenstein**: Genevese, eldest son of **Alphonse Frankenstein** and **Caroline Beaufort** (after marriage, Caroline Frankenstein); student of natural philosophy at **Ingolstadt**. His early hopes combine useful scientific discovery, fame, the elimination of disease, and making life. He becomes isolated during the creation, rejects the living being, conceals it from family and authorities, negotiates a promised mate, retracts that promise, then devotes himself to revenge (`n0010–n0013`, `n0020`, `n0027`, `n0032`, `n0035–n0037`). His declarations that he “murdered” his loved ones generally mean **moral or indirect causation**, not that he physically strangled them.
- **Alphonse Frankenstein**: Victor's father, an admired Genevan public servant and former friend of the impoverished merchant **Beaufort**. He finds Beaufort's orphaned daughter Caroline after Beaufort dies in **Lucerne**, brings her under protection, and marries her **two years later** (`n0010`). He loves and cares for Victor but cannot interpret Victor's concealed torment. He signs the letter about William as **ALPHONSE FRANKENSTEIN** (`n0015`); after Elizabeth's murder, an **apoplectic fit** follows the shock and he dies within days (`n0035`).
- **Caroline Beaufort / Madame Frankenstein**: daughter of **Beaufort**, cared for him during his poverty, then married Alphonse. She develops **scarlet fever** through nursing the recovering Elizabeth and dies before Victor departs for Ingolstadt (`n0011`). Her deathbed hope is that Victor and Elizabeth will marry. Her portrait, painted showing her mourning Beaufort, is **not the same object** as the miniature stolen from William (`n0015`).
- **Elizabeth Lavenza**: **in this supplied text, Victor's biological first cousin**, the daughter of **Alphonse's sister** and her Italian husband, taken into Alphonse and Caroline's home when Victor was four. She is raised and loved as a daughter/sister within the household and proposed as Victor's future wife (`n0010`). Thus “cousin,” “sister,” “niece,” “aunt,” and “uncle” can all occur naturally around her; **do not replace her with an unrelated adopted orphan**. She is compassionate toward Justine, writes privately to Victor about his health and the wedding, and explicitly offers to release him if he loves someone else (`n0014`, `n0016`, `n0034`). After a real wedding ceremony, she is **murdered at Evian**, not merely threatened (`n0034–n0035`).
- **Ernest Frankenstein**: Victor's younger brother, **six years younger** than Victor; unhealthy in childhood, later robust. He is nearly sixteen in Elizabeth's letter (`n0010`, `n0014`), is playing with William before the disappearance (`n0015`), and **survives** the deaths of William, Elizabeth, and Alphonse as far as the narrative explicitly reports (`n0035`). He is later expected to study in Geneva (`n0034`). Do not confuse the two brothers.
- **William Frankenstein**: the **youngest brother**, still an infant during Victor's early recollections, a child when strangled in **Plainpalais** (`n0010`, `n0015`). He wears a valuable miniature of his mother Caroline lent by Elizabeth; after discovering his surname, the creature makes William its **first murder victim** and takes the miniature (`n0026`).
- **Henry Clerval**: Victor's closest childhood friend, **son of a Geneva merchant**, not a Frankenstein sibling; fond of chivalric tales, languages, and imaginative literature. He comes to Ingolstadt unexpectedly immediately after the creature's animation, nurses Victor through a prolonged nervous fever, studies Eastern languages with him, and later travels with him through Europe (`n0010`, `n0013–n0014`, `n0030–n0031`). The creature strangles him in Ireland. A **grand jury rejects the murder charge against Victor** after his presence in the Orkneys at the relevant hour is demonstrated (`n0033`). Clerval never learns the secret of the creation.
- **Justine Moritz**: daughter of the widowed **Madame Moritz**, originally ill-treated by her mother; welcomed into the Frankenstein household as a young **servant** and educated by Caroline, whom she loves. She returns to care for her mother, then comes back after her mother's death (`n0014`). In Geneva, the miniature planted on her makes her appear to have murdered William. She is **innocent**, is convicted after circumstantial testimony and a **false confession**, and is executed (`n0015–n0016`, `n0019`). Elizabeth calls her more than a sister; distinctions of servant status and dignity are explicitly discussed in Elizabeth's letter (`n0014`).

### Other consequential figures

- **The unnamed created being**: a **sentient, articulate male** about **eight feet tall**, deliberately large because Victor finds small body parts difficult to assemble (`n0012`). Its physical features are described principally through Victor's disgust (`n0013`). Abandoned immediately after animation, it acquires language, literacy, and social ideas through observation; learns of Victor from a journal stolen or taken with clothes from the laboratory; seeks companionship; reports a sequence from kindness to violence (`n0021–n0027`). It asks for a **female counterpart**, not an existing woman. That female remains **unfinished and never animated** (`n0032`). The being later speaks again to Walton, announces remorse and the intention to destroy itself, and disappears on an ice-raft (`n0037`).
- **De Lacey / De Lacy**: the **blind French father** living in exile in a poor German cottage. The source varies the surname's spelling: **“De Lacey”** in `n0024` and **“De Lacy”** in `n0025`; record before standardizing. His children are **Felix** and **Agatha**, both initially sad and impoverished. The being observes their care for one another through a crack in the adjoining hovel. Felix helps rescue a Turkish merchant in Paris, and the family's punishment/exile follows that rescue (`n0021–n0026`).
- **Safie**: daughter of that **Turkish merchant** and a **Christian Arab mother** who had been enslaved. Her mother's teaching encourages intellectual independence; Safie resists her father's demand that she go to Constantinople, travels to the German cottage, and reunites with **Felix** (`n0023–n0024`). She is called **“the Arabian”** and **“sweet Arabian”** and learns the family's French, lessons the hidden creature follows. Her father is **not** the old blind De Lacey. Felix refers to **“my wife and my sister”** as he abandons the cottage (`n0026`): *wife* points to Safie, *sister* to Agatha, but **no wedding ceremony for Felix and Safie is actually narrated**. Do not assume a precise legal marriage date.
- **The Turkish merchant / Safie's father**: jailed and condemned in Paris, rescued by Felix; promises Safie's hand but secretly opposes her marriage to a Christian and later abandons Felix after the De Laceys' punishment (`n0024`). The narrator characterizes the case's injustice and the father's beliefs; do not turn the passage's ethnic/religious judgments into neutral external history.
- **M. Krempe** and **M. Waldman**: professors at Ingolstadt. Krempe bluntly dismisses Victor's early alchemical reading; Waldman kindly connects the ambition of earlier thinkers to modern **chemistry**, becoming Victor's mentor (`n0011–n0012`). Later, their well-meant praise reopens his horror (`n0014`). **Cornelius Agrippa, Paracelsus, Albertus Magnus** are authors Victor reads, not colleagues in the story (`n0010–n0011`).
- **Robert Walton**: self-educated would-be explorer and failed aspiring poet, age **twenty-eight** in Letter II, intensely interested in a northern passage and magnetism. His **uncle Thomas** lent him voyage books; his late father's injunction had opposed a seafaring career (`n0005–n0006`). His unnamed **lieutenant** and the gentle **master** of the ship are distinct men. Walton longs for a friend, finds one in Victor, but ultimately accepts his crew's demand to return south (`n0037`).
- **Margaret Saville**: Walton's sister, addressed as **Mrs. Saville** in England, **married and with children** by the final continuation (`n0005–n0008`, `n0037`). She does not narrate or respond on the page.
- **Mr. Kirwin**: the Irish **magistrate** who hears the suspicion against Victor after the boat's unexpected landfall, provides assistance during his sickness, writes to Alphonse, and helps secure evidence for the grand jury (`n0032–n0033`). The coastal town is **unnamed** (a dash in the text); do not assign an invented town name. **Daniel Nugent** is a witness who saw a one-man boat, not a member of the Frankenstein family (`n0033`).

## 4. Chronology and handoffs that span chapters

The frame letters print a year only as **`17—`**. The sequence of events is generally intelligible but some exact dates **contradict each other** (see §7); avoid creating a perfectly dated calendar that the text does not support.

| Sequence | Anchor | Continuity and consequences |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Walton prepares his expedition | `n0005–n0007` | Letter I: **Dec. 11**, St. Petersburgh; II: **March 28**, Archangel; III: **July 7**, at sea. He seeks discovery *and* a friend, foreshadowing Victor's ambition and loneliness. |
| Two sledges on Arctic ice | `n0008` | Walton and crew see a **gigantic** traveler heading north on a sledge, then rescue a second traveler, later identified as Victor. Letter IV is dated **Aug. 5**, with further **Aug. 13** and **Aug. 19** entries. Victor begins his account the next day. |
| Family origins, early science | `n0010–n0011` | Victor is **four** when Elizabeth joins the family; about **thirteen** when he discovers Agrippa; about **fifteen** when lightning destroys an oak; **seventeen** when he leaves for university after Caroline's death. |
| Animation and immediate abandonment | `n0012–n0014` | Following approximately **two years** of intensive science and a prolonged secret construction, Victor brings the being to life on a **November night**. The experiment is described without a reproducible mechanism. Victor flees; Clerval arrives the next morning; Victor falls ill and recovers in spring. |
| The first connected killings | `n0015–n0016` | Alphonse's letter says William vanished **May 7** and is dated **May 12**. Victor returns to Geneva after nearly **six years** away; he says the animation occurred **nearly two years earlier**. On seeing the being near the murder scene, Victor suspects it, but Justine is prosecuted and executed for the crime. |
| Glacier encounter and narrated backstory | `n0019–n0027` | In **mid-August**, almost **two months after Justine's death**, the family visits Chamounix. Victor meets the being on the glacier. The creature's account retraces the period since its November animation: forest; discovery of fire; winter shelter by the De Lacey cottage; learning words and reading; Safie's spring arrival; books found in **August**; autumn rejection; burned cottage; journey to Geneva; William's murder and Justine's framing. |
| The demand for companionship | `n0026–n0027` | The being asks for a **female being of similar kind**. Victor reluctantly agrees, conditioned on the pair's perpetual removal from the neighborhood of humanity, with the creature proposing South America. It swears to leave; the promise is **conditional**, not an accomplished departure. |
| Travel under a concealed obligation | `n0030–n0031` | Alphonse proposes Victor marry Elizabeth; Victor delays, undertakes a planned **two-year trip** with Henry, and gathers information from British scientists. Route includes the Rhine, Holland, London, English counties, Scotland, and ultimately an **unnamed small island in the Orkneys**. |
| Destruction of the second creation | `n0032` | Victor, afraid of the prospective female's free will and possible reproduction, tears apart the **inanimate, unfinished** body as the original creature watches. The being threatens to be present on Victor's **wedding-night**; Victor assumes **his own** death is intended. |
| Ireland and Henry's death | `n0032–n0033` | Victor sinks the remains at sea, is driven to an **Irish** port, discovers that Henry has been strangled, and is accused. He is held and dangerously ill, supported by Kirwin and Alphonse, then released without a full capital trial once his alibi is established. |
| Wedding, loss, revelation | `n0034–n0035` | After travel via Holyhead, Portsmouth, Havre (**May 8**), and Paris, Victor receives Elizabeth's letter dated **May 18**. They marry in Geneva, then cross the lake to **Evian**. Believing himself the intended victim, he leaves her alone; the being murders **Elizabeth**. Alphonse dies of grief/shock soon afterwards, while Ernest survives. |
| Pursuit and the Arctic frame closes | `n0035–n0037` | After months of confinement for presumed madness, Victor reports his story to a skeptical magistrate and pursues the creature via Mediterranean and **Black Sea**, **Tartary and Russia**, and Arctic ice. The being sometimes deliberately leaves clues, keeping him alive and following. The chase connects with Walton's initial sledge sighting and Victor's rescue. |
| Final shipboard events | `n0037–n0038` | Walton records entries **Aug. 26**, **Sept. 2**, **Sept. 5**, **Sept. 7**, and **Sept. 12**, with an internal date anomaly. Crew fear entrapment; Victor exhorts perseverance; Walton chooses the return route. Victor dies aboard ship. The creature speaks over his body and departs on an ice-raft, **intending** self-immolation but not shown doing it. |

### Objects and causation that should not drift between chapters

The **small portrait/miniature of Caroline** is placed on William before he disappears; the murderer takes it and slips it into a young woman's clothing, leading to **Justine's** wrongful conviction (`n0015–n0016`, `n0026`). It is **distinct** from the larger painting of Caroline kneeling by Beaufort's coffin and the separate miniature of William mentioned in Alphonse's library (`n0015`). The creature obtains **Victor's written journal of the four months before its creation** from clothing taken at the laboratory; it can therefore learn its maker and the origin of its physical form (`n0025`). Its accounts of **Safie's letters** become documentary material Victor later shows Walton (`n0024`, `n0037`). The scientist's instruments move with him to England and Orkney, but the text withholds the life-bestowing method (`n0030–n0032`, `n0037`).

## 5. Geography, social setting, and material world

- **Geneva / Lake Geneva** is Victor's home region and moral point of reference. Nearby sites have distinct functions: **Belrive** is the family's country house (`n0010`, `n0019`); **Plainpalais** is where William dies (`n0015`); **Chêne** is where Justine visited an aunt before the search (`n0016`); **Secheron** is where Victor stays outside the closed city gates (`n0015`); **Cologny** is the site of a house purchased for the newlyweds, not where Elizabeth is killed (`n0034`). Mountains and places include **Jura**, **Mont Salêve**, **Mont Blânc**, and the **Arve** valley. Source-specific spellings and accents vary and should be checked against the nodes rather than silently modernized.
- **Ingolstadt** is the university, laboratory, and animation site (`n0011–n0014`). The creature initially wanders the forest near it, then finds a **hovel attached to a cottage in Germany**; the cottage's precise town is not named (`n0021`, `n0024`). Its journey to Switzerland is a **later** move; do not put the De Lacey cottage in Geneva.
- **Chamounix, Montanvert and the glacier** are where Victor meets the articulate creature and listens to its plea. The **“sea of ice”** is the Alpine glacier in `n0020` and `n0027`, **not the Arctic sea** of the opening and closing frame. Nature often briefly comforts Victor without resolving his moral situation.
- The **De Lacey / Safie** backstory follows Paris → **Lyons** → **Mont Cenis** → **Leghorn** (the Turkish merchant's escape, involving Felix); the family is exiled from **France** to a cottage in **Germany**. Safie, who had come from **Constantinople** to Paris, later makes her own way from Italy to Germany (`n0024`). Geography is frequently understood secondhand through letters, lessons, and the creature's listening.
- Victor and Henry travel **Strasburgh → Rhine (including Manheim, Mayence, Cologne) → Rotterdam → England**, then **London → Windsor/Oxford/Matlock → Cumberland and Westmoreland → Edinburgh → Perth**; Victor travels alone through northern Scotland to a **remote Orkney island** (`n0030–n0032`). After the remains are sunk and his boat drifts, he lands in **Ireland**; he and his father later leave via **Dublin → Holyhead → Portsmouth → Havre → Paris → Geneva** (`n0032–n0034`). Elizabeth and Victor spend their wedding night in **Evian across the lake**, where she is murdered (`n0034–n0035`).
- The creature's later flight leads through the **Mediterranean**, a vessel bound for the **Black Sea**, the wilds of **Tartary and Russia**, and finally the **frozen northern ocean**; Victor follows and eventually reaches Walton's vessel (`n0036`). These are broadly narrated journey stages, not a route with enough detail to infer every intermediate location.
- **Social institutions matter.** Geneva's relatively egalitarian servant conventions are stated **by Elizabeth** when describing Justine, contrasted with her view of France and England (`n0014`). The Genevan court and religious coercion destroy Justine (`n0016`), while an Irish magistrate and grand jury eventually spare Victor (`n0033`). Safie's story combines religious, social, and gender prejudices **as represented in the narrative**, not universally valid accounts of any people (`n0024`).

## 6. Recurring diction, images, books, and motifs

**Creator, family, responsibility.** The central asymmetry is between **making a sentient being** and **accepting obligations to it**. Victor imagines a grateful new species and himself as a superior father (`n0012`), then instantly abandons his creation (`n0013`). The being rebukes its creator, invokes its rights, and likens itself to **Adam** and **the fallen angel** (`n0020`, `n0025`, `n0027`). The *Paradise Lost* epigraph, repeated on the volume title pages (`n0002`, `n0018`, `n0029`), frames this grievance before the story begins. In the novel, the being itself reads *Paradise Lost* as if it were historical truth, alternately identifying with Adam and Satan (`n0025`). These contrasts are not interchangeable decorative religious epithets.

**Epithets and referents.** **“Frankenstein” usually denotes Victor**, sometimes his family name. The being is described by *Victor* and sometimes others as **creature, being, monster, dæmon, devil, fiend, wretch, enemy**, etc.; the creature also calls itself degraded names. These choices register **speaker, emotional temperature, and moral stance**; do not flatten them into one stable proper name or make every hostile epithet an objective description. The difference between **creator/creature**, **master/slave**, and **father/child** can invert within dialogue: the being declares itself Victor's “master” in the Orkneys (`n0032`), reversing the imagined authority of `n0012`.

**Knowledge, education and speech.** Victor's attraction to alchemy, then electricity, modern chemistry and physiology traces the ambivalent power of knowledge (`n0010–n0012`). **Natural philosophy** is the text's historical umbrella term; it should not be narrowed automatically to today's physics. The childhood **lightning-shattered oak** demonstrates his early response to electricity, **not** an explicit description of the mechanism that animates the body (`n0010`, `n0012–n0013`). The being is likewise educated: it distinguishes the senses; learns fire's helpful and harmful powers; picks up **French** from the De Lacey household and Safie's lessons; becomes literate; and infers social rank, law, gender, and exclusion (`n0021–n0025`). Knowledge for both can enlarge sympathy **and** sharpen despair.

**Books and quotations.** Victor's early reading includes **Cornelius Agrippa**, **Paracelsus**, and **Albertus Magnus**, while Waldman gives him a different account of scientific progress (`n0010–n0011`). Felix reads **Volney's *Ruins of Empires*** to teach Safie, through which the creature encounters comparative history and human injustice (`n0023`). The creature finds **a volume of *Plutarch's Lives***, **the *Sorrows of Werter*** (use the spelling of the supplied text), and ***Paradise Lost*** (`n0025`). Verse, allusions, and self-interrupting quotations in Walton's letters and Victor's telling—including the albatross reference, the fearful walker, Alpine mutability, and the poetry of nature—have distinct narrative functions (`n0006`, `n0013`, `n0020`, `n0030`). Preserve meaningful quotation boundaries, attributed vs. unattributed status, and changes of voice; do not import explanatory quotations from another edition.

**Light, fire, seasons, weather.** The original ambition is to bring the **light of knowledge** into the world; creation takes place by dim candlelight on a wet November night (`n0012–n0013`). Fire first **warms**, then **burns** the creature, and finally destroys the De Lacey cottage (`n0021`, `n0026`). Seasonal cycles track its growth from winter exposure through spring hope and autumn loss, and Victor's temporary relief during natural excursions; they are not merely scenic background (`n0014`, `n0019–n0026`). The **sublime** Alps and the increasingly lethal polar environment both supply moments of awe and threats to life (`n0005`, `n0020`, `n0036–n0037`). In the finale the creature imagines a **funeral pyre**, turning beneficial and destructive fire toward its own body—but the burning itself is **not witnessed** (`n0037`).

**Friendship, exclusion and reciprocal vengeance.** Walton longs for a peer and believes Victor might be his ideal friend (`n0006–n0008`). Victor relies on Clerval's practical devotion but hides his central secret (`n0013–n0014`). The creature calls the unknowing De Lacey family friends and is rejected when seen; its demand for a partner is expressed as a precondition for peace, not as proof that peace would necessarily follow (`n0025–n0027`). The figure of a **lonely wanderer or outcast** repeats in all three principal narrators. After the creature destroys the wedding, Victor's revenge pursuit mirrors the creature's earlier pursuit of Victor (`n0032–n0037`). The text repeatedly contrasts **justice, retribution, vengeance, duty, useful work**, and the obligation not to injure others: distinguish a speaker's moral argument from the novel's settled verdict.

## 7. Ambiguities and editorial watchpoints

| Issue | What the source says, and how to handle it |
| --- | --- |
| **Elizabeth's origin is edition-specific** | This supplied 1818-form account unequivocally makes her **Alphonse's niece and Victor's cousin** (`n0010`); descriptions of her as sister/daughter reflect **upbringing**, not unrelated parentage. Do not harmonize with other versions. |
| **Wedding-night threat** | The being warns Victor it will be present on his wedding night (`n0032`). Victor assumes **it will kill him** (`n0032`, `n0034`); the actual victim is **Elizabeth** (`n0035`). Preserve the gap between knowledge and misunderstanding, a crucial suspense device. |
| **Safie and Felix's status** | Their intended marriage is discussed, and Felix later calls Safie **“my wife”** (`n0024`, `n0026`), but the narrative describes no ceremony. The phrase may indicate a relationship changed off-page or a loose designation; do not invent an explicit marriage scene. |
| **Who framed Justine?** | The creature says it placed the portrait in an **unnamed woman's** clothing after William's death (`n0026`); Justine is the woman arrested with the portrait (`n0015–n0016`). This is a strongly supported connection across chapters. The precise moment at which the fabric's folds became the **pocket** named in the trial is not explained and need not be invented. |
| **The female creature's agency** | Her potential refusal of the promised pact, possible hostility to the first creature, and possible offspring are **Victor's fears about a being never brought to life** (`n0032`), not events that occur. Her body is dismembered before animation; there is no living “bride.” |
| **Science is not a how-to** | Victor claims he discovered how to give life but withholds the principle from Walton (`n0012`, `n0037`). The novel includes lightning, electricity, anatomy, charnel houses, and instruments; it **does not explicitly describe a lightning-powered reanimation procedure**. Do not insert adaptation-specific machinery. |
| **Partial evidence and self-interested testimony** | Walton considers Felix/Safie's letters and a sledge sighting supporting evidence, but none independently demonstrates every incident (`n0037`). Victor's accounts of the being's malice and culpability, the being's accounts of prior virtue and remorse, and Walton's admiration are **situated testimony**. The being admits the murders yet challenges the idea that its suffering excuses them (`n0026`, `n0037`). |
| **Spirits and providence** | During the pursuit, Victor attributes food, water, dreams, and resolve to supernatural ministering spirits (`n0036`). This is his **belief**, not independently confirmed divine intervention. His last advice to Walton both warns against ambition and momentarily allows that **another might succeed** (`n0037`); keep that tension. |
| **De Lacey/De Lacy** | The supplied text uses both spellings (`n0024–n0025`). Treat any normalization as an explicitly tracked editorial decision, not an unnoticed correction. Other historical spellings, accents, typographical quirks, and archaic usage likewise belong to the source record. |
| **English arrival dates** | Volume III Chapter I places Victor's first sight of Britain's cliffs in the **latter days of December** (`n0030`); the following chapter says **“We had arrived in England at the beginning of October, and it was now February”** (`n0031`). These conflict. A translator should retain both source statements or flag an edition-level decision; **do not silently reconcile them**. |
